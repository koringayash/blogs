# Latent Variables & the EM Algorithm

*Part V · Unsupervised. K-Means (Chapter 12) assigned every point to one cluster with certainty. This chapter drops the certainty: a point may belong to several clusters with different probabilities, and the algorithm that handles that is called EM.*

## 1. Everyday picture: two coins in one bag

You have two coins, A and B, with unknown biases (their chances of landing heads). A friend picks one coin at random, flips it $10$ times, and tells you only **how many heads** came up. They repeat this five times. You are **never told which coin** was used in which round.

1. If you **knew** which coin was used each time, estimating the biases would be easy: for each coin, divide its heads by its flips.
2. If you **knew the biases**, you could make a good guess about which coin was used: a round with $9$ heads is probably the coin with the larger bias.
3. You know neither, and each unknown needs the other. This is the same chicken-and-egg structure as K-Means.

EM breaks the circle exactly as K-Means did, with one change:
- **Guess the biases.**
- **E-step:** for each round, work out the **probability** that each coin was used (not a hard yes/no).
- **M-step:** re-estimate each bias, counting every round **in proportion to** that probability.
- Repeat.

Section 4 works this example through with real numbers.

## 2. The setting and the difficulty

**What we have.**
1. **Observed data** $X=\{\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_N\}$.
2. **Latent (hidden) variables** $Z=\{z_1,\dots,z_N\}$, one per data point. In this chapter each $z_n$ takes one of $K$ discrete values (which cluster, which coin).
3. **Parameters** $\theta$ that we want to estimate.

**The model is written as a joint distribution.** For independent data points,

$$ P(X,Z\mid\theta)=\prod_{n=1}^{N}P(\mathbf{x}_n,z_n\mid\theta) $$ {#eq-joint}

**What maximum likelihood needs.** We only observe $X$, so we must maximize the probability of $X$ alone. Obtain it by **summing out** the hidden variable:

$$ P(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}P(\mathbf{x}_n,z_n=k\mid\theta) $$ {#eq-marginal}

Taking the logarithm of the product over independent points gives the **observed-data log-likelihood**:

$$ \log P(X\mid\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{K}P(\mathbf{x}_n,z_n=k\mid\theta)\Big) $$ {#eq-loglik-obs}

### Why this is hard: the logarithm of a sum

Compare with the case where the hidden variables **were** observed. Then the **complete-data log-likelihood** is

$$ \log P(X,Z\mid\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log P(\mathbf{x}_n,z_n\mid\theta) $$ {#eq-loglik-complete}

| | Complete data, [[eq-loglik-complete]] | Observed data, [[eq-loglik-obs]] |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | log of a **single** term | log of a **sum** of $K$ terms |
| Can the log split the term apart? | Yes: a log of a product is a sum of logs | No: $\log(a+b)$ has no simple form |
| Result | The parameters separate into small, easy problems | All parameters are tangled together inside one logarithm |
| Closed-form maximizer? | Usually yes | Usually **no** |

**In the coin example:** if we knew the coin used in each round, the log-likelihood would split into one term for coin A's flips and one for coin B's flips, each maximized by a simple "heads over flips" ratio. With the coin unknown, each round contributes $\log(\text{coin A term}+\text{coin B term})$, and the two coins' parameters cannot be separated.

### The gradient shows what goes wrong

Differentiate one term of [[eq-loglik-obs]] with respect to $\theta$:

$$ \nabla_\theta\log\sum_kP(\mathbf{x}_n,k\mid\theta)=\frac{\sum_k\nabla_\theta P(\mathbf{x}_n,k\mid\theta)}{\sum_jP(\mathbf{x}_n,j\mid\theta)}=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\underbrace{\frac{P(\mathbf{x}_n,k\mid\theta)}{P(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\theta)}}_{=\,P(z_n=k\mid\mathbf{x}_n,\theta)}\;\nabla_\theta\log P(\mathbf{x}_n,k\mid\theta) $$ {#eq-grad-id}

:::box step | How we got here
- The first equality is the chain rule for the logarithm: the derivative of $\log u$ is $u'/u$, and the sum in the numerator is $u'$.
- For the second, use $\nabla P=P\,\nabla\log P$ on each term, and note that the denominator is [[eq-marginal]].
- The ratio $\dfrac{P(\mathbf{x}_n,k\mid\theta)}{P(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\theta)}$ is, by Bayes' rule, the posterior probability that point $n$ came from component $k$.
:::

:::box plain | What is this identity saying in plain words?
- The gradient of the hard log-of-a-sum is a **weighted average of the easy complete-data gradients**, with weights equal to the posterior probabilities of the hidden variable.
- Those weights depend on $\theta$ itself. Setting the gradient to zero therefore gives an equation in which $\theta$ appears on **both sides**: it cannot be solved for $\theta$ in one line.
- This suggests a plan: **freeze the weights at their current values, then solve the easy weighted problem**. That plan is the EM algorithm.
:::

:::figure F13.5 | Log of a sum vs sum of logs | For the same two-component model, the complete-data log-likelihood (one clear peak) drawn beside the observed-data log-likelihood (several bumps and ridges). A slider changes the amount of data, showing that the observed curve stays awkward.

## 3. The EM algorithm

Start from some initial parameters $\theta^{(0)}$. Then repeat two steps, with $\theta^{(t)}$ the current estimate.

### E-step (Expectation): fill in the hidden variables with probabilities

Using the current parameters, compute the posterior probability that each point belongs to each component. This is Bayes' rule:

$$ \gamma_{nk}=P\big(z_n=k\mid\mathbf{x}_n,\theta^{(t)}\big)=\frac{P\big(z_n=k\mid\theta^{(t)}\big)\;P\big(\mathbf{x}_n\mid z_n=k,\theta^{(t)}\big)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}P\big(z_n=j\mid\theta^{(t)}\big)\;P\big(\mathbf{x}_n\mid z_n=j,\theta^{(t)}\big)} $$ {#eq-gamma}

:::box plain | What is this saying in plain words?
- **Numerator:** how plausible is it that component $k$ was chosen (prior) **and** that it produced this point (likelihood).
- **Denominator:** the same quantity added up over all components, so the $\gamma_{nk}$ for a fixed point add up to $1$.
- $\gamma_{nk}$ is called the **responsibility** of component $k$ for point $n$, or a **soft assignment**. It is a probability, replacing the hard $0$ or $1$ of K-Means.
:::

### M-step (Maximization): re-estimate the parameters, using the responsibilities as weights

We cannot maximize the observed log-likelihood, but we can maximize the **expected complete-data log-likelihood**. This is the average of the easy quantity [[eq-loglik-complete]], where the hidden variables are averaged using the probabilities from the E-step:

$$ Q\big(\theta,\theta^{(t)}\big)=\mathbb{E}_{Z\mid X,\theta^{(t)}}\Big[\log P(X,Z\mid\theta)\Big] $$ {#eq-Q-def}

Because $z_n$ is discrete and the data points are independent, the expectation becomes a sum with weights $\gamma_{nk}$:

$$ Q\big(\theta,\theta^{(t)}\big)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{nk}\,\log P\big(\mathbf{x}_n,z_n=k\mid\theta\big) $$ {#eq-Q}

:::box step | How we got here
- Insert [[eq-loglik-complete]] into [[eq-Q-def]]. The expectation of a sum over $n$ is the sum of the expectations.
- For one point, the expectation over the discrete variable $z_n$ is $\sum_kP(z_n=k\mid\mathbf{x}_n,\theta^{(t)})\,\log P(\mathbf{x}_n,z_n=k\mid\theta)$, and the probability is $\gamma_{nk}$ from [[eq-gamma]].
- The $\gamma_{nk}$ are **fixed numbers** during the M-step (they come from $\theta^{(t)}$). Only the $\log P(\cdot\mid\theta)$ factor depends on the new $\theta$.
:::

Then the new parameters are

$$ \theta^{(t+1)}=\arg\max_{\theta}\;Q\big(\theta,\theta^{(t)}\big) $$ {#eq-mstep}

:::box plain | What is the M-step saying in plain words?
- [[eq-Q]] is the complete-data log-likelihood of [[eq-loglik-complete]], except that **every point is counted $K$ times, once per component, with weight $\gamma_{nk}$**.
- That is a **weighted** version of the easy problem, so it usually has a closed-form solution.
- **Pretend our uncertainty about the hidden labels is represented by the probabilities $\gamma_{nk}$, and fit the model to that weighted data.**
:::

**The algorithm in one list:**
1. Choose initial parameters $\theta^{(0)}$.
2. **E-step:** compute all responsibilities $\gamma_{nk}$ with [[eq-gamma]].
3. **M-step:** compute $\theta^{(t+1)}$ by maximizing [[eq-Q]], as in [[eq-mstep]].
4. Repeat steps 2 and 3 until the log-likelihood (or the parameters) stops changing.

## 4. A worked example: two coins

**The data.** Five rounds of $10$ flips. The number of heads in each round is

$$ h_1,\dots,h_5=5,\;9,\;8,\;4,\;7 $$

**The model.**
- The hidden variable $z_n\in\{A,B\}$ is the coin used in round $n$.
- Each coin is equally likely to be picked: $P(z_n=A)=P(z_n=B)=\tfrac12$ (assumed known).
- Coin $k$ lands heads with probability $\theta_k$. The parameters are $\theta=(\theta_A,\theta_B)$.
- Given the coin, the probability of a particular sequence with $h$ heads and $10-h$ tails is $\theta_k^{\,h}(1-\theta_k)^{10-h}$.

**Start** with the guess $\theta_A=0.6$ and $\theta_B=0.5$.

### E-step for this model

Insert the model into [[eq-gamma]]. The prior $\tfrac12$ appears in both terms and cancels, and so would the binomial coefficient if we counted sequences by their number of heads:

$$ \gamma_{nA}=\frac{\theta_A^{\,h_n}(1-\theta_A)^{10-h_n}}{\theta_A^{\,h_n}(1-\theta_A)^{10-h_n}+\theta_B^{\,h_n}(1-\theta_B)^{10-h_n}},\qquad\gamma_{nB}=1-\gamma_{nA} $$ {#eq-gamma-coin}

For round 1 ($h=5$): the numerator is $0.6^5\,0.4^5=0.000796$ and the other term is $0.5^{10}=0.000977$, so $\gamma_{1A}=\dfrac{0.000796}{0.000796+0.000977}=0.449$. The full table, rounded to two decimals:

| Round $n$ | Heads $h_n$ | $\gamma_{nA}$ | $\gamma_{nB}$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 5 | $0.45$ | $0.55$ |
| 2 | 9 | $0.80$ | $0.20$ |
| 3 | 8 | $0.73$ | $0.27$ |
| 4 | 4 | $0.35$ | $0.65$ |
| 5 | 7 | $0.65$ | $0.35$ |

Read this as: the round with $9$ heads is $80\%$ likely to have used coin A (the coin with the larger current bias), and the round with $4$ heads is only $35\%$ likely.

### M-step for this model

Here $\log P(\mathbf{x}_n,z_n=k\mid\theta)=\log\tfrac12+h_n\log\theta_k+(10-h_n)\log(1-\theta_k)$, so [[eq-Q]] becomes, up to a constant that does not involve $\theta$,

$$ Q=\sum_{n}\Big[\gamma_{nA}\big(h_n\log\theta_A+(10-h_n)\log(1-\theta_A)\big)+\gamma_{nB}\big(h_n\log\theta_B+(10-h_n)\log(1-\theta_B)\big)\Big] $$ {#eq-Q-coin}

The two coins separate, so maximize each alone. For coin A, differentiate with respect to $\theta_A$ and set to zero:

$$ \frac{\sum_n\gamma_{nA}h_n}{\theta_A}-\frac{\sum_n\gamma_{nA}(10-h_n)}{1-\theta_A}=0\;\Longrightarrow\;\theta_A=\frac{\sum_n\gamma_{nA}\,h_n}{10\sum_n\gamma_{nA}} $$ {#eq-mstep-coin}

(and the same formula with $\gamma_{nB}$ for coin B).

:::box plain | What is this saying in plain words?
- The numerator is the **expected number of heads** produced by coin A, where each round contributes in proportion to the chance that it was coin A.
- The denominator is the **expected number of flips** of coin A.
- So $\theta_A$ is "expected heads over expected flips", the same "heads over flips" ratio as in the case where we knew the coins, with **fractional counts**.
:::

**Numbers for coin A** (using the rounded table): expected heads $=0.45\cdot5+0.80\cdot9+0.73\cdot8+0.35\cdot4+0.65\cdot7=21.24$, expected flips $=10\,(0.45+0.80+0.73+0.35+0.65)=29.8$. So $\theta_A\approx21.24/29.8=0.71$. For coin B, using $\gamma_{nB}$: expected heads $=11.76$ and expected flips $=20.2$, so $\theta_B\approx0.58$.

**Continuing the same two steps** (computed without rounding):

| Iteration | $\theta_A$ | $\theta_B$ | Observed log-likelihood, up to a constant |
|---|---|---|---|
| start | $0.600$ | $0.500$ | not tabulated |
| 1 | $0.713$ | $0.581$ | $-31.86$ |
| 2 | $0.745$ | $0.569$ | $-31.72$ |
| 3 | $0.768$ | $0.550$ | $-31.63$ |
| 5 | $0.791$ | $0.526$ | $-31.57$ |
| 10 | $0.797$ | $0.520$ | $-31.57$ |
| 30 | $0.797$ | $0.520$ | $-31.57$ |

**What we see:**
- The two estimates **move apart**: the algorithm decides that the high-head rounds come from one coin and the low-head rounds from the other.
- The log-likelihood **never decreases**, as Section 5 proves.
- It **settles** at a fixed point, here about $(0.80,\,0.52)$.

An E-step button updates the splits, an M-step button updates the two bias estimates, and a small plot tracks the log-likelihood rising.

## 5. Why it works: Jensen's inequality and a lower bound

EM has to earn its keep. Why should maximizing the *expected complete-data* log-likelihood improve the thing we actually care about, [[eq-loglik-obs]]?

**Step 1: write the hard term with an arbitrary distribution.** For each point $n$, pick any probability distribution $q_n(k)$ over the components (so $q_n(k)\ge0$ and $\sum_kq_n(k)=1$), and multiply and divide by it inside the sum:

$$ \log P(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\theta)=\log\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_n(k)\,\frac{P(\mathbf{x}_n,z_n=k\mid\theta)}{q_n(k)} $$ {#eq-multiply}

The inside of the logarithm is now an **average** of the quantities $a_k=\dfrac{P(\mathbf{x}_n,k\mid\theta)}{q_n(k)}$, with weights $q_n(k)$.

**Step 2: apply Jensen's inequality.** The logarithm is a concave function. For a concave function, the function of an average is **at least** the average of the function, the rule of [Chapter 7](chapter.html?c=07-decision-trees#eq-jensen):

$$ \log\Big(\sum_kq_k\,a_k\Big)\;\ge\;\sum_kq_k\,\log a_k $$ {#eq-jensen-em}

**Step 3: the lower bound.** Apply [[eq-jensen-em]] to [[eq-multiply]] and sum over points:

$$ \log P(X\mid\theta)\;\ge\;\mathcal{L}(q,\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}q_n(k)\,\log\frac{P(\mathbf{x}_n,z_n=k\mid\theta)}{q_n(k)} $$ {#eq-lower-bound}

:::box plain | What is the bound saying in plain words?
- The hard quantity (the log of a sum) is **at least** as large as this easier quantity (a sum of logs), **whichever** probabilities $q_n(k)$ we choose.
- So $\mathcal{L}(q,\theta)$ is a **floor** under the log-likelihood. Pushing the floor up pushes the log-likelihood up with it, **provided** the floor stays in contact with it.
- EM is a method for raising the floor, then making sure it touches the true curve.
:::

**Step 4: when does the bound touch the true value?** Jensen's inequality is an equality exactly when the quantity inside, $a_k$, is **the same for every $k$**. That means

$$ \frac{P(\mathbf{x}_n,z_n=k\mid\theta)}{q_n(k)}=c_n\quad\text{for all }k\;\Longrightarrow\;q_n(k)\;\propto\;P(\mathbf{x}_n,z_n=k\mid\theta) $$

Since the $q_n(k)$ must add up to $1$, the constant is fixed, and

$$ q_n(k)=\frac{P(\mathbf{x}_n,z_n=k\mid\theta)}{\sum_jP(\mathbf{x}_n,z_n=j\mid\theta)}=P\big(z_n=k\mid\mathbf{x}_n,\theta\big) $$ {#eq-tight}

**This is exactly the E-step.** The best choice of $q_n$ is the posterior probability $\gamma_{nk}$ of [[eq-gamma]], and with that choice the bound is **tight**: $\mathcal{L}(q,\theta^{(t)})=\log P(X\mid\theta^{(t)})$.

**Step 5: the M-step raises the floor.** Keep $q_n(k)=\gamma_{nk}$ fixed and split [[eq-lower-bound]] into two parts:

$$ \mathcal{L}(q,\theta)=\underbrace{\sum_{n,k}\gamma_{nk}\log P(\mathbf{x}_n,z_n=k\mid\theta)}_{Q(\theta,\theta^{(t)})}\;\underbrace{-\sum_{n,k}\gamma_{nk}\log\gamma_{nk}}_{\text{does not depend on }\theta} $$ {#eq-split-bound}

The second part is the **entropy** of the responsibilities (compare [Chapter 7](chapter.html?c=07-decision-trees#eq-entropy)). It contains no $\theta$, so **maximizing $\mathcal{L}$ over $\theta$ is the same as maximizing $Q$**. That is [[eq-mstep]], the M-step.

**Step 6: put the two steps together.** One full round of EM satisfies:

$$ \log P\big(X\mid\theta^{(t+1)}\big)\;\underset{\text{(a)}}{\ge}\;\mathcal{L}\big(q^{(t)},\theta^{(t+1)}\big)\;\underset{\text{(b)}}{\ge}\;\mathcal{L}\big(q^{(t)},\theta^{(t)}\big)\;\underset{\text{(c)}}{=}\;\log P\big(X\mid\theta^{(t)}\big) $$ {#eq-monotone-em}

:::box step | How we got here
- **(a)** The lower bound [[eq-lower-bound]] holds for every $\theta$, including the new one.
- **(b)** The M-step picked $\theta^{(t+1)}$ to **maximize** $\mathcal{L}(q^{(t)},\cdot)$, so it is at least as high as at the old $\theta^{(t)}$.
- **(c)** The E-step chose $q^{(t)}$ to make the bound **tight** at $\theta^{(t)}$, by [[eq-tight]].
- **Conclusion: the observed log-likelihood never decreases from one round to the next.**
:::

:::figure F13.2 | EM as lower-bound climbing | The log-likelihood curve over a single parameter. At the current theta the E-step draws a lower-bound curve that touches it. The M-step jumps to the peak of that lower curve, and the likelihood at the new point is higher. The next E-step draws a new lower curve, and the process climbs step by step.

:::tabs Three ways to see the E-step and the M-step
--- Climbing with a rope
You are on a foggy hillside (the log-likelihood) and cannot see far. The E-step builds a **small, easy hill** that lies entirely below the real one and **touches it where you stand**. The M-step walks to the top of the small hill. Since the small hill is below the real one, the real hill is at least as high there. Repeat.
--- Filling in the blanks
If the hidden labels were known, the problem would be easy. The E-step fills the blanks with **soft guesses** (probabilities). The M-step solves the easy problem as if those soft guesses were real, using fractional counts. Better parameters give better guesses, and better guesses give better parameters.
--- Alternating maximization
Treat $q$ and $\theta$ as two groups of unknowns, and $\mathcal{L}(q,\theta)$ as the objective. The E-step maximizes it over $q$ (with $\theta$ fixed), and the M-step maximizes it over $\theta$ (with $q$ fixed). Each is an exact maximization, so the objective cannot decrease. This is the same argument as for [K-Means](chapter.html?c=12-kmeans#eq-monotone).
:::

### A second view: the gap is a KL divergence

Use $P(\mathbf{x}_n,k\mid\theta)=P(k\mid\mathbf{x}_n,\theta)\,P(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\theta)$ inside [[eq-lower-bound]]. The factor $P(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\theta)$ comes out of the logarithm, and the sum of $q_n(k)$ is $1$, leaving

$$ \log P(X\mid\theta)=\mathcal{L}(q,\theta)+\sum_{n=1}^{N}\mathrm{KL}\Big(q_n\;\Big\|\;P(z_n\mid\mathbf{x}_n,\theta)\Big),\qquad\mathrm{KL}(q\,\|\,p)=\sum_kq(k)\log\frac{q(k)}{p(k)}\ge0 $$ {#eq-kl}

So the **gap** between the log-likelihood and the bound is the total KL divergence between $q_n$ and the true posterior. The KL divergence is never negative, which reproduces the inequality [[eq-lower-bound]], and it is exactly zero when $q_n$ equals the posterior, which reproduces [[eq-tight]].

## 6. Where EM stops

**Fixed points are stationary points of the likelihood.** Apply the gradient identity [[eq-grad-id]] and compare it with the gradient of $Q$ at $\theta=\theta^{(t)}$ (where the weights are exactly the $\gamma_{nk}$ of [[eq-gamma]]):

$$ \nabla_\theta Q\big(\theta,\theta^{(t)}\big)\Big|_{\theta=\theta^{(t)}}=\nabla_\theta\log P\big(X\mid\theta\big)\Big|_{\theta=\theta^{(t)}} $$ {#eq-fixed}

:::box step | How we got here
- Differentiate [[eq-Q]]: $\sum_{n,k}\gamma_{nk}\nabla_\theta\log P(\mathbf{x}_n,k\mid\theta)$.
- Differentiate [[eq-loglik-obs]] using [[eq-grad-id]]: $\sum_{n,k}P(k\mid\mathbf{x}_n,\theta)\nabla_\theta\log P(\mathbf{x}_n,k\mid\theta)$.
- At $\theta=\theta^{(t)}$ the two weights coincide.
- So if the M-step cannot move $\theta$ (the gradient of $Q$ is zero there), the gradient of the log-likelihood is zero too.
:::

**What this means.**
1. EM stops at a **stationary point** of the likelihood: a local maximum, or occasionally a saddle point.
2. It does **not** guarantee the global maximum.
3. The log-likelihood increases at every step, but it can **crawl** when it is close to a flat region.

**Practical consequences:**
- **Initialization matters**, as in K-Means. Run EM from several starting points and keep the one with the highest final log-likelihood. A common trick is to initialize with the result of K-Means.
- **Monitor the log-likelihood.** It must not decrease. If it does, there is a bug.
- **Watch for degenerate solutions.** In Gaussian mixtures one component can collapse onto a single point and make the likelihood blow up. [Chapter 14](chapter.html?c=14-gmm) discusses this.

## 7. Hard versus soft clustering, and EM contains K-Means

| | Hard clustering ([K-Means](chapter.html?c=12-kmeans)) | Soft clustering (EM) |
|---|---|---|
| Latent variable | $r_{nk}\in\{0,1\}$, exactly one is $1$ | $\gamma_{nk}\in[0,1]$, adding to $1$ |
| A point halfway between two clusters | forced to pick one | $\gamma\approx(0.5,0.5)$, honest about the uncertainty |
| Update step | mean of the assigned points | **weighted** mean, weights $\gamma_{nk}$ |
| Objective | distortion $J$, never increases | log-likelihood, never decreases |
| Cluster shapes | round, equal | whatever the model allows (Chapter 14) |

:::figure F13.3 | Hard vs soft assignment | One point dragged between two cluster centres. A toggle switches between K-Means (the point flips colour at the midline) and EM (the point's colour blends smoothly, with its responsibilities shown as two bars).

**K-Means as a limiting case of EM.** Consider a mixture of $K$ spherical Gaussians with the **same** small variance $\varepsilon$, equal mixing weights, and centres $\boldsymbol\mu_k$. The responsibility [[eq-gamma]] is then

$$ \gamma_{nk}=\frac{\exp\!\big(-\lVert\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k\rVert^2/2\varepsilon\big)}{\sum_j\exp\!\big(-\lVert\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_j\rVert^2/2\varepsilon\big)} $$ {#eq-softmax}

**Take $\varepsilon\to0$.** Compare the nearest centre with any other. The ratio of their responsibilities is $\exp\!\big(-(d_k^2-d_{\text{near}}^2)/2\varepsilon\big)$, where $d_k=\lVert\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k\rVert$. This tends to $0$ for every centre that is **not** the nearest. So $\gamma_{nk}\to1$ for the nearest centre and $\to0$ for all others.

:::box plain | What does this mean in plain words?
- As the clusters get very tight, the responsibilities become one-hot: **EM's E-step turns into K-Means's assignment to the nearest centre**.
- The weighted mean in the M-step then becomes a plain mean over the assigned points, which is [K-Means's update](chapter.html?c=12-kmeans#eq-mean).
- **K-Means is "hard EM" with round clusters of equal size.** The full derivation for general Gaussians is the subject of [Chapter 14](chapter.html?c=14-gmm).
:::

## 8. When should you use EM?

**Use EM when both of these hold:**
1. The model has **hidden variables** (or missing data), so that maximizing the likelihood directly means a log of a sum.
2. **If the hidden variables were known, the maximization would be easy** (closed form), and the **posterior over the hidden variables can be computed** (the E-step).

**Common models solved with EM:**

| Model | Hidden variable | What the E-step computes |
|---|---|---|
| [Gaussian mixture model](chapter.html?c=14-gmm) | which Gaussian produced each point | responsibilities $\gamma_{nk}$ |
| Hidden Markov model (Baum-Welch algorithm) | the hidden state at each time | posterior over states, via the forward-backward algorithm |
| Topic models (pLSA) | the topic of each word | posterior over topics |
| Missing data in regression or covariance estimation | the missing values | their expected values given the observed ones |
| Mixtures of experts, mixtures of regressions | which expert handles each point | responsibilities |

**When not to use it, or what to watch for:**
- If the posterior over the hidden variables **cannot be computed**, the exact E-step fails, and variants such as variational EM or Monte Carlo EM approximate it.
- If direct gradient-based maximization of the likelihood is easy, it may be simpler (and by [[eq-grad-id]] the gradients are the same quantities that EM uses).
- EM can be **slow** near the end, and it gets **stuck in local maxima**, so use several restarts.
- EM **maximizes the likelihood only**. For a fully Bayesian treatment you would keep a whole distribution over the parameters (this leads to variational inference).

## 1. Everyday picture: two overlapping crowds

Imagine measuring the heights of everyone who walks into a station.
- The heights look like **one lumpy shape**, but the crowd is really **two groups mixed together**, for example children with a school trip and adults.
- Each group, taken alone, has a bell-shaped (Gaussian) spread of heights, with its own average and its own spread.
- You never see which group a person belongs to, you see only their height. A person of medium height could come from either group.

A **Gaussian mixture model (GMM)** is exactly this description:
1. The data is a **mixture** of $K$ Gaussian groups.
2. Each group has its own **mean** (where it sits), **covariance** (its shape and size), and **mixing weight** (how large a share of the crowd it makes up).
3. Which group produced each point is a **latent variable**, as in [Chapter 13](chapter.html?c=13-em).

Compared with [K-Means](chapter.html?c=12-kmeans), a GMM:
- lets clusters be **ellipses of different sizes and orientations**, not only equal round balls,
- lets a point belong to **several clusters with probabilities**, not just one,
- is a proper **probability model** of the data, so we can compute likelihoods, generate new points, and spot unusual ones.

## 2. The model

### The mixture density

**Step 1: one Gaussian.** In $D$ dimensions, a Gaussian with mean $\boldsymbol\mu$ and covariance $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ has density

$$ \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma)=\frac{1}{(2\pi)^{D/2}\lvert\boldsymbol\Sigma\rvert^{1/2}}\exp\!\Big(-\tfrac12(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)\Big) $$ {#eq-gaussian}

**Step 2: mix $K$ of them.** The GMM density is a weighted sum of $K$ Gaussians:

$$ P(\mathbf{x}\mid\theta)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k),\qquad0\le\pi_k\le1,\quad\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k=1 $$ {#eq-gmm}

All the parameters together are

$$ \theta=\big\{\boldsymbol\mu_k,\;\boldsymbol\Sigma_k,\;\pi_k\;:\;k=1,\dots,K\big\} $$ {#eq-theta}

:::box plain | What is this model saying in plain words?
- $\pi_k$ is **how common** group $k$ is. The constraint that the $\pi_k$ add up to $1$ makes the whole thing a valid probability density.
- $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ is **where** group $k$ sits, and $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k$ is **how it is spread** (its width in each direction, and how those directions tilt).
- The density of a point is the **weighted sum** of what each group would give it.
:::

### The latent variable

**Step 1: a one-hot hidden label.** For each point introduce $\mathbf{z}=(z_1,\dots,z_K)^\top$ with exactly one entry equal to $1$, as in [Chapter 12's 1-of-$K$ coding](chapter.html?c=12-kmeans#eq-onehot). The entry $z_k=1$ means "this point was generated by component $k$". For $K=3$ the three possibilities are $(1,0,0)$, $(0,1,0)$ and $(0,0,1)$.

**Step 2: the prior over the label.** We do not know $\mathbf{z}$, so we give it probabilities, the mixing weights:

$$ P(z_k=1)=\pi_k,\qquad\boldsymbol\pi=(\pi_1,\dots,\pi_K)^\top,\quad\sum_k\pi_k=1 $$ {#eq-prior}

**Step 3: the point given its label.** If component $k$ was chosen, the point is drawn from that component's Gaussian:

$$ P(\mathbf{x}\mid z_k=1)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k) $$ {#eq-cond}

**Step 4: the joint and the marginal.** Multiply, then sum out the label (the sum has one nonzero term for each possible value of $\mathbf{z}$):

$$ P(\mathbf{x},z_k=1)=\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k),\qquad P(\mathbf{x})=\sum_{k=1}^{K}P(\mathbf{x},z_k=1)=\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k) $$ {#eq-joint-gmm}

:::box step | How we got here
- The joint is "prior times conditional", from [[eq-prior]] and [[eq-cond]].
- Summing over which component was used, as in [Chapter 13's marginal](chapter.html?c=13-em#eq-marginal), gives back exactly the mixture density [[eq-gmm]].
- So the mixture density is the density of data produced by a **two-stage process**: first pick a group by $\boldsymbol\pi$, then draw the point from that group's Gaussian.
:::

**For the whole data set**, every point $\mathbf{x}_n$ has its own hidden label $\mathbf{z}_n$, but **all points share the same $\boldsymbol\pi$** and the same component parameters. Given the labels, the points are independent:

$$ P(\mathbf{x}_1,\dots,\mathbf{x}_N\mid\mathbf{z}_1,\dots,\mathbf{z}_N)=\prod_{n=1}^{N}P(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\mathbf{z}_n) $$ {#eq-factor}

## 3. The likelihood, and why there is no formula

The data points are independent draws, so the likelihood is the product of the mixture densities, and the log-likelihood is a sum:

$$ \log P(X\mid\theta)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)\Big) $$ {#eq-loglik-gmm}

This is [Chapter 13's observed-data log-likelihood](chapter.html?c=13-em#eq-loglik-obs) for the Gaussian mixture. The **logarithm of a sum** sits in front of us again, so the parameters cannot be separated and there is no closed-form maximizer. The Chapter 13 plan applies: use EM, which needs the responsibilities (the E-step) and the weighted updates (the M-step).

## 4. The E-step: responsibilities

**Step 1: what we want.** For point $\mathbf{x}_n$, the probability that it came from component $k$, given the data and the current parameters. This is the posterior of the latent variable.

**Step 2: Bayes' rule.**

$$ \gamma_{nk}=P(z_{nk}=1\mid\mathbf{x}_n)=\frac{P(z_k=1)\,P(\mathbf{x}_n\mid z_k=1)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}P(z_j=1)\,P(\mathbf{x}_n\mid z_j=1)} $$ {#eq-bayes-gmm}

**Step 3: insert the model.** Use [[eq-prior]] and [[eq-cond]]:

$$ \gamma_{nk}=\frac{\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)}{\sum_{j=1}^{K}\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j)} $$ {#eq-gamma-gmm}

:::box plain | What is this saying in plain words?
- **Numerator:** how common component $k$ is, times how well its Gaussian explains this particular point.
- **Denominator:** the same quantity added over all components, so that the $K$ numbers for one point add up to $1$.
- $\gamma_{nk}$ is the **responsibility** of component $k$ for point $n$, written $r_{nk}$ in your handwritten notes. It is a **soft assignment**.
- A point close to the centre of one group gets a responsibility near $1$ for that group. A point between two groups gets a split, such as $0.6$ and $0.4$.
:::

Also define the **effective number of points** that component $k$ is responsible for. It is the sum of its responsibilities:

$$ N_k=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\gamma_{nk},\qquad\sum_{k=1}^{K}N_k=N $$ {#eq-Nk}

(The second statement holds because $\sum_k N_k=\sum_n\sum_k\gamma_{nk}=\sum_n1=N$.) In K-Means, $N_k$ would be the plain count of points in cluster $k$. Here it can be fractional.

## 5. The M-step: deriving the three updates

We want the parameters that maximize the log-likelihood [[eq-loglik-gmm]], with the responsibilities treated as known numbers. We differentiate with respect to each group of parameters and set the result to zero.

### A shortcut that does all the hard work once

Take one data point and a parameter $\phi_k$ that belongs **only** to component $k$ (its mean or its covariance). Only the $k$-th term of the sum inside the logarithm depends on $\phi_k$:

$$ \frac{\partial}{\partial\phi_k}\log\sum_{j}\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j)=\frac{\pi_k\,\dfrac{\partial\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)}{\partial\phi_k}}{\sum_j\pi_j\,\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j)}=\gamma_{nk}\;\frac{\partial\,\log\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)}{\partial\phi_k} $$ {#eq-lemma}

:::box step | How we got here
- The first equality is the chain rule for the logarithm ($u'/u$), keeping only the term that contains $\phi_k$.
- For the second, use $\dfrac{\partial\mathcal{N}}{\partial\phi}=\mathcal{N}\,\dfrac{\partial\log\mathcal{N}}{\partial\phi}$, and recognize $\dfrac{\pi_k\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j)}$ as $\gamma_{nk}$ from [[eq-gamma-gmm]].
- This is [Chapter 13's gradient identity](chapter.html?c=13-em#eq-grad-id) for the mixture.
:::

The logarithm of the Gaussian is easy to differentiate, because it is a simple quadratic:

$$ \log\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}\mid\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma)=-\tfrac{D}{2}\log2\pi-\tfrac12\log\lvert\boldsymbol\Sigma\rvert-\tfrac12(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu) $$ {#eq-loggaussian}

### Update 1: the means $\boldsymbol\mu_k$

**Step 1: differentiate [[eq-loggaussian]] with respect to $\boldsymbol\mu$.** Only the last term depends on $\boldsymbol\mu$, and the derivative of $\tfrac12(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)$ with respect to $\boldsymbol\mu$ is $-\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)$ (the same kind of derivative as in [Chapter 2](chapter.html?c=02-linear-regression#eq-grad)). So

$$ \frac{\partial\log\mathcal{N}}{\partial\boldsymbol\mu}=\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu) $$

**Step 2: add up over the data, using [[eq-lemma]].**

$$ \frac{\partial\log P(X\mid\theta)}{\partial\boldsymbol\mu_k}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\gamma_{nk}\,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k) $$ {#eq-dmu}

**Step 3: set it to zero and solve.** Multiply by $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k$ (invertible) to remove it, then split the sum:

$$ \sum_n\gamma_{nk}\mathbf{x}_n=\boldsymbol\mu_k\sum_n\gamma_{nk}\;\Longrightarrow\;\boldsymbol\mu_k^{\text{new}}=\frac{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\gamma_{nk}\,\mathbf{x}_n}{\sum_{n=1}^{N}\gamma_{nk}}=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\gamma_{nk}\,\mathbf{x}_n $$ {#eq-mu}

:::box plain | What is this saying in plain words?
- The new mean of component $k$ is the **average of all the points, where each point counts in proportion to how responsible component $k$ is for it**.
- A point that belongs almost entirely to another component hardly moves this mean.
- Compare with [K-Means](chapter.html?c=12-kmeans#eq-mean): there, a point counted fully ($1$) in one cluster and not at all ($0$) in the others. Here it counts by a fraction between $0$ and $1$.
:::

### Update 2: the covariances $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k$

**Step 1: the two matrix-derivative facts we need** (standard results for a symmetric $\boldsymbol\Sigma$):

$$ \frac{\partial\log\lvert\boldsymbol\Sigma\rvert}{\partial\boldsymbol\Sigma}=\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1},\qquad\frac{\partial\,\mathbf{a}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{a}}{\partial\boldsymbol\Sigma}=-\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\mathbf{a}\mathbf{a}^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1} $$ {#eq-matrix-facts}

**Step 2: differentiate [[eq-loggaussian]] with respect to $\boldsymbol\Sigma$.** Apply [[eq-matrix-facts]] with $\mathbf{a}=\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu$:

$$ \frac{\partial\log\mathcal{N}}{\partial\boldsymbol\Sigma}=-\tfrac12\Big[\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}-\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma^{-1}\Big] $$ {#eq-dlogN}

**Step 3: add up over the data using [[eq-lemma]].**

$$ \frac{\partial\log P(X\mid\theta)}{\partial\boldsymbol\Sigma_k}=-\tfrac12\sum_{n}\gamma_{nk}\Big[\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}-\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}\Big] $$ {#eq-dSigma}

**Step 4: collect terms.** The first piece sums to $N_k\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}$ by [[eq-Nk]]. Pull the common factors $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}$ out of the second piece:

$$ \frac{\partial\log P}{\partial\boldsymbol\Sigma_k}=-\tfrac12N_k\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}+\tfrac12\,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1}\Big[\sum_n\gamma_{nk}(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top\Big]\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{-1} $$ {#eq-dSigma2}

**Step 5: set to zero and solve.** Multiply on the left and on the right by $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k$ and rearrange:

$$ N_k\boldsymbol\Sigma_k=\sum_n\gamma_{nk}(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top\;\Longrightarrow\;\boldsymbol\Sigma_k^{\text{new}}=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\gamma_{nk}\,(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top $$ {#eq-Sigma}

Here $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ is the **new mean** from [[eq-mu]]. (The covariance update is done after the mean update, which is the correct order because the mean is where the scatter should be measured from.)

:::box plain | What is this saying in plain words?
- The covariance of an ordinary data set is the average of $(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)(\mathbf{x}-\boldsymbol\mu)^\top$. This is the **same thing, with every point weighted by its responsibility**, and divided by the total weight $N_k$.
- Each $(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)(\mathbf{x}_n-\boldsymbol\mu_k)^\top$ is a matrix that is large in the direction the point is away from the centre. Adding them up gives the **shape and tilt** of the cloud of points belonging to component $k$.
- It is a symmetric positive semi-definite matrix, so it is a valid covariance (given enough points, it is positive definite).
:::

### Update 3: the mixing weights $\pi_k$

The $\pi_k$ have a **constraint**, $\sum_k\pi_k=1$, so we add a Lagrange multiplier $\lambda$ for it:

$$ \mathcal{J}=\log P(X\mid\theta)+\lambda\Big(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\pi_k-1\Big) $$ {#eq-lagrange-pi}

**Step 1: differentiate the log-likelihood with respect to $\pi_k$.** Only the $k$-th term of the inner sum depends on $\pi_k$, with derivative $\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$. Then use $\gamma_{nk}=\pi_k\mathcal{N}_{nk}\big/\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal{N}_{nj}$, so that $\mathcal{N}_{nk}\big/\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal{N}_{nj}=\gamma_{nk}/\pi_k$:

$$ \frac{\partial\mathcal{J}}{\partial\pi_k}=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)}{\sum_j\pi_j\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_j,\boldsymbol\Sigma_j)}+\lambda=\frac{N_k}{\pi_k}+\lambda $$ {#eq-dpi}

**Step 2: set it to zero.** This gives $\pi_k=-N_k/\lambda$.

**Step 3: find $\lambda$ from the constraint.** Set $\partial\mathcal{J}/\partial\lambda=\sum_k\pi_k-1=0$. Add up $\pi_k=-N_k/\lambda$ over $k$ and use $\sum_kN_k=N$ from [[eq-Nk]]:

$$ 1=\sum_k\pi_k=-\frac{N}{\lambda}\;\Longrightarrow\;\lambda=-N $$

**Step 4: the update.**

$$ \pi_k^{\text{new}}=\frac{N_k}{N} $$ {#eq-pi}

:::box plain | What is this saying in plain words?
- $\pi_k$ is **the fraction of the data that component $k$ is responsible for**: its total responsibility divided by the total number of points.
- This is the natural estimate of "how common is group $k$?", counting each point fractionally.
- Because it is a fraction of the total, the weights automatically add up to $1$, which is what the Lagrange multiplier enforced.
:::

:::tabs Three ways to see the update formulas
--- Weighted statistics
Each update is an ordinary statistic with weights. [[eq-mu]] is a **weighted mean**, [[eq-Sigma]] is a **weighted covariance**, and [[eq-pi]] is a **weighted fraction**. The weights are the responsibilities.
--- Tug of war
Imagine each component as a magnet that pulls the points it is responsible for. A point's pull on a magnet is proportional to its responsibility. The magnet slides to the weighted centre of what it pulls, and its field stretches in the direction of its pulled points.
--- Complete-data fitting
If the labels were known, fitting a Gaussian to each group would be the textbook estimates: mean, covariance and fraction of the data. The M-step does the same with **fractional labels** $\gamma_{nk}$, as in [Chapter 13's M-step](chapter.html?c=13-em#eq-mstep).
:::

The mean is marked as the weighted centre, the covariance as an ellipse around it, and pi as the share of the total weight, with a slider changing how much weight a chosen point carries.

## 6. The same thing as EM's M-step, and the whole algorithm

**Why this is exactly the EM M-step.** [Chapter 13](chapter.html?c=13-em#eq-Q) defined $Q(\theta,\theta^{(t)})=\sum_{n,k}\gamma_{nk}\log P(\mathbf{x}_n,z_{nk}=1\mid\theta)$. For the GMM, [[eq-joint-gmm]] gives

$$ Q\big(\theta,\theta^{(t)}\big)=\sum_{n=1}^{N}\sum_{k=1}^{K}\gamma_{nk}\Big[\log\pi_k+\log\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{x}_n\mid\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)\Big] $$ {#eq-Q-gmm}

Differentiating [[eq-Q-gmm]] gives **the same** expressions as [[eq-dmu]], [[eq-dSigma]] and [[eq-dpi]], because the factor $\gamma_{nk}$ that [[eq-lemma]] produced is already sitting in front of each term. So maximizing $Q$ gives [[eq-mu]], [[eq-Sigma]] and [[eq-pi]].

:::box warn | An important subtlety
- If we treated the $\gamma_{nk}$ in [[eq-mu]], [[eq-Sigma]] and [[eq-pi]] as functions of the **new** parameters, these would be implicit equations, with the unknowns on both sides. This is why there is no single closed form for the maximum of [[eq-loglik-gmm]].
- EM solves this by **computing the $\gamma_{nk}$ from the old parameters and then holding them fixed**. That gives explicit formulas, and [Chapter 13's guarantee](chapter.html?c=13-em#eq-monotone-em) tells us that each such round cannot lower the log-likelihood.
- The updates give the true maximizer of $Q$, because for fixed weights, fitting a Gaussian is the standard weighted maximum-likelihood problem.
:::

**The algorithm:**
1. **Initialize** $\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k,\pi_k$. A common choice is to run K-Means first, then use the cluster means, the cluster covariances and the cluster fractions.
2. **E-step:** compute all responsibilities $\gamma_{nk}$ with [[eq-gamma-gmm]], using the current parameters.
3. **M-step:** compute $N_k$ with [[eq-Nk]], then update $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ with [[eq-mu]], then $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k$ with [[eq-Sigma]], then $\pi_k$ with [[eq-pi]].
4. **Evaluate** the log-likelihood [[eq-loglik-gmm]]. It must not decrease. Stop when it (or the parameters) change by less than a small tolerance.

**Cost per round.** Evaluating $K$ Gaussians at $N$ points costs roughly $O(NKD^2)$ once each covariance has been factorized, plus about $O(KD^3)$ for the factorizations. The M-step has the same order. In practice, work with logarithms of the densities and use the "log-sum-exp" trick to avoid numbers that underflow to zero.

## 7. A worked example (one dimension)

Five points: $x=1,\,2,\,4,\,9,\,10$. Use $K=2$ and start from $\pi_1=\pi_2=0.5$, $\mu_1=2$, $\mu_2=8$, and $\sigma_1^2=\sigma_2^2=4$. In one dimension the covariance is just a variance $\sigma^2$.

### The first E-step

For $x=1$: the first component's term is $0.5\cdot\mathcal{N}(1\mid2,4)=0.5\cdot\dfrac{e^{-1/8}}{\sqrt{8\pi}}=0.0880$, and the second is $0.5\cdot\dfrac{e^{-49/8}}{\sqrt{8\pi}}=0.00022$. So $\gamma_{11}=\dfrac{0.0880}{0.0880+0.00022}=0.9975$. All five points:

| $x_n$ | $\gamma_{n1}$ | $\gamma_{n2}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $0.9975$ | $0.0025$ |
| 2 | $0.9890$ | $0.0110$ |
| 4 | $0.8176$ | $0.1824$ |
| 9 | $0.0025$ | $0.9975$ |
| 10 | $0.0006$ | $0.9994$ |

The point $x=4$ is mostly, but not completely, assigned to the left component. That is the "soft" part.

### The first M-step

Effective counts by [[eq-Nk]]: $N_1=0.9975+0.9890+0.8176+0.0025+0.0006=2.807$, so $N_2=5-2.807=2.193$.

- **Weights** by [[eq-pi]]: $\pi_1=2.807/5=0.561$, $\pi_2=0.439$.
- **Mean of component 1** by [[eq-mu]]: $\mu_1=\dfrac{0.9975\cdot1+0.9890\cdot2+0.8176\cdot4+0.0025\cdot9+0.0006\cdot10}{2.807}=\dfrac{6.274}{2.807}\approx2.23$.
- The second mean and the variances are computed the same way, using [[eq-Sigma]] in one dimension: $\sigma_k^2=\frac{1}{N_k}\sum_n\gamma_{nk}(x_n-\mu_k)^2$.

### Running the same two steps

| Round | $\pi_1,\pi_2$ | $\mu_1,\mu_2$ | $\sigma_1^2,\sigma_2^2$ | Log-likelihood |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| start | $0.500,\ 0.500$ | $2.00,\ 8.00$ | $4.00,\ 4.00$ | $-12.558$ |
| 1 | $0.561,\ 0.439$ | $2.23,\ 9.00$ | $1.52,\ 2.85$ | $-11.353$ |
| 2 | $0.596,\ 0.404$ | $2.32,\ 9.45$ | $1.55,\ 0.54$ | $-9.977$ |
| 3 | $0.600,\ 0.400$ | $2.33,\ 9.50$ | $1.56,\ 0.25$ | $-9.736$ |
| 10 | $0.600,\ 0.400$ | $2.33,\ 9.50$ | $1.56,\ 0.25$ | $-9.736$ |

**What we see:**
- The log-likelihood **never decreases**, as [Chapter 13](chapter.html?c=13-em#eq-monotone-em) guarantees.
- The algorithm settles after three rounds. The point $x=4$ ends up fully assigned to the left component, which therefore holds three points and has weight $\pi_1=3/5=0.6$.
- The final parameters are just the ordinary mean and variance of each group. For the points $\{1,2,4\}$ they are $\mu=7/3\approx2.33$ and $\sigma^2=14/9\approx1.56$, and for $\{9,10\}$ they are $\mu=9.5$ and $\sigma^2=0.25$.
- **The variance of the second component shrank from $4$ to $0.25$.** A GMM can give different groups different spreads, which K-Means cannot.

## 8. Practical issues

### Problem 1: a component can collapse onto a single point

Suppose $\boldsymbol\mu_k$ sits exactly on one data point $\mathbf{x}_n$ and $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k$ shrinks towards zero. In one dimension, the Gaussian's value at its own mean is $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_k}$, which tends to **infinity** as $\sigma_k\to0$. That one point's term in [[eq-loglik-gmm]] becomes arbitrarily large, so **the log-likelihood has no upper limit**. The "best" solution is a useless spike on one point.

**Remedies:**
1. **Regularize** the covariances, for example add a small multiple of the identity: $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k\leftarrow\boldsymbol\Sigma_k+\epsilon\mathbf{I}$.
2. **Detect and reset:** if a component's $N_k$ becomes tiny or its covariance nearly singular, re-initialize it somewhere else.
3. **Use a prior** on the covariances (a MAP version of EM), the same idea as [Ridge in Chapter 3](chapter.html?c=03-ridge-lasso#eq-ridge).
4. **Restrict the covariance** to a simpler form (the next table).

This problem does **not** arise in K-Means, which has a built-in lower limit on its cost.

### Problem 2: initialization and local maxima

EM only finds a local maximum ([Chapter 13](chapter.html?c=13-em#eq-fixed)). Run it from several starts, or initialize with K-Means, and keep the run with the highest log-likelihood.

### Problem 3: the shape of the covariance

A full covariance matrix has many parameters when $D$ is large. Simpler choices trade flexibility for stability:

| Covariance type | Form | Parameters per component | Cluster shape |
|---|---|---|---|
| Spherical | $\sigma_k^2\mathbf{I}$ | $1$ | round, with its own size |
| Diagonal | $\operatorname{diag}(\sigma_{k1}^2,\dots,\sigma_{kD}^2)$ | $D$ | axis-aligned ellipse |
| Tied | one $\boldsymbol\Sigma$ shared by all components | $D(D+1)/2$ in total | same ellipse for every component |
| Full | $\boldsymbol\Sigma_k$ unrestricted | $D(D+1)/2$ | any ellipse, any tilt |

The total number of free parameters for $K$ components with full covariances is

$$ p=K\,D+K\,\frac{D(D+1)}{2}+(K-1) $$ {#eq-params}

(the $K-1$ counts the mixing weights, which must sum to $1$). This is a bias-variance choice, as in [Chapter 4](chapter.html?c=04-bias-variance): more freedom fits the data better but needs more data and risks overfitting.

### Problem 4: choosing $K$

More components always raise the maximum likelihood, so we cannot choose $K$ by the likelihood alone (the same issue as in [Chapter 12](chapter.html?c=12-kmeans#eq-J)). Because a GMM is a probability model, there is a principled criterion: the **Bayesian information criterion**

$$ \text{BIC}=-2\log P(X\mid\hat\theta)+p\,\ln N $$ {#eq-bic}

where $p$ is the number of parameters from [[eq-params]]. Fit the model for several values of $K$ and choose the $K$ with the **smallest** BIC. The second term charges for complexity. Cross-validated likelihood on held-out data is another option.

### Problem 5: the labels are interchangeable

Swapping the names of components 1 and 2 gives exactly the same model. So the "component numbers" have no meaning on their own, which can confuse comparisons between runs. Compare the fitted **densities** or the groupings, not the labels.

## 9. What you can do with a fitted GMM

1. **Soft clustering:** the responsibilities $\gamma_{nk}$ of [[eq-gamma-gmm]] tell you how strongly each point belongs to each group. For a hard assignment, pick $\arg\max_k\gamma_{nk}$.
2. **Classify a new point:** apply [[eq-gamma-gmm]] to it with the fitted parameters.
3. **Density estimation and anomaly detection:** evaluate $P(\mathbf{x})$ from [[eq-gmm]]. A very small value marks an unusual point.
4. **Generate new data:** pick a component with probability $\pi_k$, then draw from $\mathcal{N}(\boldsymbol\mu_k,\boldsymbol\Sigma_k)$ (the two-stage process of Section 2).
5. **Use the means and covariances** as a compact summary of the data's structure.

## 10. GMM versus K-Means

| | K-Means ([Chapter 12](chapter.html?c=12-kmeans)) | GMM |
|---|---|---|
| Model of the data | none, only a distortion cost | a probability density, [[eq-gmm]] |
| Assignment | hard, $r_{nk}\in\{0,1\}$ | soft, $\gamma_{nk}\in[0,1]$, [[eq-gamma-gmm]] |
| Cluster shape | round balls, equal size | ellipses, free size and tilt |
| Cluster weights | implicit in the counts | explicit $\pi_k$, [[eq-pi]] |
| Mean update | plain mean of assigned points | weighted mean, [[eq-mu]] |
| Covariance | none | weighted covariance, [[eq-Sigma]] |
| Objective | distortion, never increases | log-likelihood, never decreases |
| Parameters | $K\cdot D$ | [[eq-params]] |
| Degenerate solution | no | yes, collapsed components |
| Cost per round | $O(NKD)$ | about $O(NKD^2)$ |

**Recovering K-Means.** Fix every covariance to $\varepsilon\mathbf{I}$ with the same $\varepsilon$ for all components, fix equal weights $\pi_k=1/K$, and let $\varepsilon\to0$. By [Chapter 13's limit](chapter.html?c=13-em#eq-softmax), the responsibilities become one-hot, the E-step becomes "nearest centre", and [[eq-mu]] becomes the plain mean of the assigned points. So **K-Means is a special, hard-assignment limit of the Gaussian mixture model.**
